📐 Reference

Model Notes

What changed between v1.0 (the book's Appendix C) and v1.1 (the current tools), and what it means for the model's claims.

📋 The Short Version

v1.0 (book, Appendix C) assumed the debt path was fixed, then computed each bond type's coupons against it. This understated the debt accumulation effect of higher coupons.

v1.1 (current tools) computes a separate debt path for each bond type using its own coupons. This captures the feedback loop the book identifies in Chapter 14: higher coupons → higher debt service → more borrowing → higher future debt service.

The numbers differ, but the direction of every claim still holds — except distress, which is slightly lower in v1.1 than in v1.0. The book's warning about the feedback loop was correct, but the fixed-debt model overstated the magnitude.

🧭 What This Means for the Book's Claims

✅ Cost reduction (~3.1%) — Still holds. The productivity bond is cheaper than a conventional bond on expected cost, regardless of model version.
✅ Feedback loop — Still holds, and is now visible in the tool. Higher coupons in good years increase the debt stock, which increases future debt service. The book's identification of this as the central design problem was correct.
⚠️ Productivity distress (88.17%) — The v1.1 number is 85.68%, not 88.17%. The book's number was from the fixed-debt model, which overstates the effect because it doesn't let the debt stock adjust per bond type. The v1.1 number is more realistic.
⚠️ Inflation-linked comparison — ILB outperforms the productivity bond on both cost (0.4896 vs 0.4994) and distress (67% vs 86%) under the default calibration. The book frames the productivity bond as the primary alternative to conventional, but on cost-minimisation grounds, an ILB is a cheaper answer. The case for the productivity bond rests on alignment with efficiency metrics — not on cost minimisation. This distinction is important and should be explicit in any policy discussion.
⚠️ θ_AI — The book treats θ_AI as the single most important parameter, with thresholds at 0.15 and 0.30. In the current calibration, AI shocks are rare (5% per year) and modest, so θ_AI affects outcomes primarily through those shocks. The thresholds are from the book's analytical framework, not from the simulation. A simulation with larger or more frequent AI shocks would give θ_AI more influence.
📊 Optimal portfolio share — The book cites ~30%. The v1.1 model shows this is scenario-dependent: 0% for high-debt sovereigns (60%+), 30–45% for moderate debt (30–50%). The optimal share is a property of the specific calibration, not a universal recommendation.

❓ Common Objections

"Doesn't the ILB make the productivity bond pointless?" ▼

No. An ILB protects against inflation. A productivity bond rewards efficiency. If you believe efficiency is the metric we should be rewarding — because it's what we want the economy to produce, not just what we want to protect against — then the productivity bond is the more appropriate instrument. The ILB is a defensive tool; the productivity bond is a strategic one. They serve different purposes and can coexist.

"The book says 88.17% distress but the tool says 85.68%. Which is right?" ▼

Both are correct for their respective models. The book's number is from the fixed-debt model; the tool's is from the endogenous-debt model. The endogenous model is more realistic and is the current production calibration. The difference is 2.5 percentage points — small enough that the qualitative conclusion (distress rises under variable coupons without a PAM) is unchanged, but large enough that any specific numeric claim in policy discussion should cite which model produced it.

"Isn't the whole model just a simulation, not evidence?" ▼

Correct. The simulation tests whether the architecture can work under specified assumptions; it does not prove it will work in reality. The assumptions — about AI shocks, about government capture, about investor behaviour — matter more than the simulation itself. Every one of those assumptions should be challenged before any real-world implementation. The simulation is a tool for designing the instrument, not a forecast of the outcome.

"What's actually different between v1.0 and v1.1 in code terms?" ▼

In v1.0, state_generator.py returned a debt path of zeros. The debt stock was passed in as a fixed baseline and used only for computing debt service — it never grew. In v1.1, compute_debt_path() takes a coupon series and rolls forward the debt stock year by year. Each bond type gets its own debt path, so the feedback loop is captured. The GitHub repo (traffictorch/productivity-bond-model) has both models in git history for verification.
